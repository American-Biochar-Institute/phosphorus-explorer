Phosphorus in biochar, by feedstock

Biochar retains much of the phosphorus in its source biomass, so phosphorus content varies widely by feedstock. Wood biochar typically carries only a few pounds of P2O5 per ton, while some manure and biosolids biochars carry more than 100 pounds per ton. This explorer presents every phosphorus value the American Biochar Institute has extracted from its reference library, along with the feedstock, measurement reagent or digestion, and source study, plus commercial products measured for the USDA-ARS Biochar Atlas. Filters recalculate the statistics, and each figure explains what the data can and cannot support.

This page serves users who need the underlying data rather than a summary, including producers and agronomists assessing applications, researchers, product manufacturers, and nutrient-rule writers. It supports USDA-NRCS Conservation Practice Standard 336, Soil Carbon Amendment, by providing evidence for considering feedstock-specific phosphorus values and for evaluating ABI’s proposal to treat up to 25 pounds of P2O5 per acre from unfortified biochar as an incidental contribution, counted cumulatively over any rolling three-year period.

This page shows how much phosphorus different types of biochar contain, how much may be available to crops, and why the results depend on the feedstock and testing method. Readers can compare the evidence, explore how the values change with different filters, and see the application-rate and source-coefficient approaches ABI proposes for nutrient planning.

Why the feedstock is the whole story

Phosphorus that leaves a field can degrade water quality, so nutrient rules limit how much may be applied and when a plan is required. Biochar made from wood typically carries a few pounds of P₂O₅ per dry ton. Biochar made from manure or biosolids can carry a hundred or more. Both are sold as biochar. That is why the figures below are reported by feedstock class and why a pooled average across classes describes the mix of studies in the library rather than a property of biochar. What each reagent recovers

Each reagent extracts a different phosphorus pool, so the results are neither additive nor interchangeable. Water represents the readily soluble fraction. Sodium bicarbonate, used in the Olsen test and the bicarbonate step of Hedley fractionation, and Mehlich-3 are soil tests for plant-available phosphorus, but they were calibrated for soils rather than carbon-rich amendments. Dilute HCl and the Hedley acid step extract calcium-bound phosphorus. Two percent citric acid, two percent formic acid and neutral ammonium citrate come from fertilizer and phosphate-rock reactivity testing and recover most of the phosphorus present. Because interpretation depends on the reagent used, every row records the extractant.

How much phosphorus an application actually delivers

Phosphorus applied per acre depends on the biochar's concentration and how much of it goes on: lb P₂O₅/acre = total P (mg/kg dry) × dry short tons/acre × 0.004582. The line marks 25 lb P₂O₅ per acre, the figure ABI proposes as an incidental contribution from unfortified biochar, counted cumulatively over any rolling three-year period. What a published source-coefficient relationship returns for biochar

Some states use a phosphorus source coefficient to adjust a material’s phosphorus score based on its expected runoff potential, so equal amounts from manure and fertilizer are not treated alike. No coefficient has been established for biochar. This figure applies the Elliott et al. 2006 relationship, PSC = 0.102 × WEP0.99, calibrated on raw manures and biosolids with a 200:1 water extraction, to each biochar in the dataset with an absolute water-extractable phosphorus value. Shaded bands show published coefficient ranges for raw manures and biosolids as a reference. The figure responds to the feedstock and wood-subset filters. These results are not source coefficients.
