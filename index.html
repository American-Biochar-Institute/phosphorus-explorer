Phosphorus in biochar, by feedstock

Biochar carries phosphorus inherited from the biomass it was made from, and how much depends almost entirely on the feedstock. Wood biochar and manure biochar differ by roughly fiftyfold, so a single number for "biochar" misleads. This page holds every phosphorus value the American Biochar Institute has extracted from its reference library, with the feedstock it came from, the reagent that measured it, and the study it is printed in. Filtering the set recomputes the statistics, and each figure states what its data will and will not support.

It is published for anyone who needs the underlying numbers rather than a summary: producers and agronomists weighing an application, researchers, product manufacturers, and the people who write nutrient rules. It was assembled to support ABI's comments on the proposed revision of USDA-NRCS Conservation Practice Standard 336, Soil Carbon Amendment (Docket NRCS-2026-0100).

Why the feedstock is the whole story

Phosphorus that leaves a field can degrade water quality, so nutrient rules limit how much may be applied and when a plan is required. Biochar made from wood typically carries a few pounds of P₂O₅ per dry ton. Biochar made from manure or biosolids can carry a hundred or more. Both are sold as biochar. That is why the figures below are reported by feedstock class and why a pooled average across classes describes the mix of studies in the library rather than a property of biochar. What each reagent recovers

Extractable phosphorus by reagent. Each reagent recovers a different pool, so these values are neither additive nor interchangeable. Water approximates the immediately soluble fraction. Sodium bicarbonate (the Olsen test, and the bicarbonate step of a Hedley fractionation) and Mehlich-3 are agronomic soil tests for plant-available phosphorus, calibrated on soils rather than on a carbon-rich amendment. Dilute HCl and the Hedley acid step dissolve calcium-bound phosphorus. Two percent citric acid, two percent formic acid and neutral ammonium citrate come from the fertilizer and phosphate-rock reactivity lineage rather than from soil testing, and recover most of what is present. What a value means depends on which reagent produced it, so the extractant is recorded on every row.

How much phosphorus an application actually delivers

Phosphorus applied per acre depends on the biochar's concentration and how much of it goes on: lb P₂O₅/acre = total P (mg/kg dry) × dry short tons/acre × 0.004582. The line marks 25 lb P₂O₅ per acre, the figure ABI has asked NRCS to treat as an incidental contribution from wood biochar, counted cumulatively over three years. What a published source-coefficient relationship returns for biochar

A phosphorus source coefficient is a planning factor some States use to scale a material's phosphorus by how readily it is thought to run off, so that a pound in manure and a pound in fertilizer are not scored alike. None exists for biochar. This figure shows what the Elliott et al. 2006 relationship, PSC = 0.102 × WEP0.99, returns when applied to every biochar in this dataset carrying an absolute water-extractable value. The shaded bands are published coefficient ranges for unpyrolyzed materials, shown for scale. This figure responds to the feedstock and woody-subset filters. Not source coefficients
